RW

Hi, I’m Remko Weijnen.

I’m an Innovation Engineer at Cloudhouse Labs, where I work on early product ideas, prototypes and technical experiments before they become formal engineering projects. My role sits close to product: exploring what might be useful, building enough to prove or disprove an idea, and helping turn future product direction into something people can actually see, test and discuss.

I’ve spent nearly thirty years working in and around Windows, infrastructure, virtualization, application delivery and end-user computing. Earlier in my career I worked on infrastructure migration projects, including moves from Windows NT-era environments to Active Directory, Exchange and Citrix-based platforms. I have always used development and automation to make consultancy and administration work more reliable, repeatable and less boring — what many people would now simply call DevOps.

Over time, application delivery became my main focus. A lot of that work involved making difficult or badly behaved applications run properly on VDI and SBC platforms without weakening security or turning the environment into a collection of exceptions. That naturally led me deeper into Windows internals, debugging, reverse engineering and application compatibility.

I was a Citrix CTP from 2013 until the programme ended in 2025, have been a Parallels VIPP since 2017, and co-founded the Dutch Citrix User Group.

These days my work and interests also include security research, reverse engineering, automation, AI-assisted development, Home Assistant, homelab setups and building small tools to solve specific problems.

This blog is where I write about the things I work on and the technical rabbit holes I end up in. Some posts are polished walkthroughs. Others are closer to lab notes: things I tried, broke, reversed, automated or eventually understood well enough to write down.

Expect posts about Windows internals, application delivery, debugging, security tooling, reverse engineering, packaging difficult applications, AI workflows, automation, Home Assistant, lab setups and the occasional obscure problem future-me would otherwise forget.

I write mostly for people who enjoy practical technical detail: engineers, sysadmins, packagers, security folks, homelabbers and anyone who likes understanding how things work under the hood.

Nothing here is meant to be perfect or final. It is a technical blog, not a product manual. But when I find something useful, strange or hard-won, I try to write it down.

Privacy

Last updated: 10 November 2026

This is a personal blog. I don’t use advertising or tracking, the site itself sets no cookies, and the only statistics I keep are anonymous reading counts, which you can switch off (see below). This section describes what data is processed when you read it, and by whom.

Hosting: GitHub Pages and Cloudflare

The blog is a static site. The pages are hosted on GitHub Pages (GitHub, Inc., part of Microsoft) and reach you through Cloudflare, which sits in front of it as a CDN and for security.

Like any web server, both see the technical details of each request: your IP address, the page you ask for, your browser’s user agent, the referring page and the time. They use this to deliver the pages and to protect the site against abuse. I don’t receive these logs myself. Cloudflare gives me aggregated statistics, such as the number of requests per page and which crawlers (including AI crawlers) visit the site, but not your IP address. AI services may read the posts to answer questions; crawlers that only collect training data for parties that don’t link back are refused, both in robots.txt and at Cloudflare.

Cloudflare may set a strictly necessary security cookie (cf_clearance) if it shows you a security check. The site itself sets no cookies.

Both companies are based in the United States and are certified under the EU-US Data Privacy Framework. Their own privacy statements apply: GitHub and Cloudflare. The legal basis is my legitimate interest in publishing the blog and keeping it secure.

Reading statistics

Why. I write these posts in my own time, and I want to know which ones actually help people. Page counts alone don’t tell me that: a post can be opened often and abandoned after one paragraph. So I look at which posts are read to the end, where readers give up halfway (often a sign that a post is unclear, too long or out of date), and which topics bring readers in from other sites. That decides what I write next, which old posts I update or fix, and where a follow-up is worth the time.

What. Each post page has a small script of my own. When you leave a post, it sends one message to the site with: the address of the post, its number of words, whether the end of the post was on your screen, how far down the post you got (as a percentage), how many seconds the post was on your screen (time in a background tab doesn’t count), how many of those you were actively reading (some scrolling, typing or pointer movement in the last 30 seconds), and the name of the website that linked you here, if it was another site (for example google.com). When you followed a link I shared myself that carries a ?ref= tag (for example ?ref=linkedin), it sends that tag instead, so I can see which of my shares brought readers. These tags only ever name a channel, such as a social network or a newsletter, never a person. Nothing else: no cookie, no identifier, and no IP address or browser details are stored, so the counts can’t be linked to you or to each other.

Your choice. You can switch reading statistics off under Reading statistics in the site settings (the gear icon, or the menu on a phone); your browser then sends nothing. If your browser sends Global Privacy Control or Do Not Track, the script sends nothing at all, without you having to do anything.

The counts are received by a Cloudflare Worker and stored in a Cloudflare D1 database that is restricted to the European Union. Only I can see them. I also look at Google Search Console, which tells me how often Google showed and clicked the posts, and at Cloudflare’s aggregated counts of which known crawlers (search engines, AI services) fetched each post; neither comes from your browser. The legal basis is my legitimate interest in knowing which posts are useful.

Feedback on posts

Newer posts end with a link to a short feedback form. Why: for the same reason as the reading statistics, but in your own words: whether a post solved your problem, whether its level and length were right, and what you were actually looking for. That tells me what to explain better, what to update and what to write next.

What: only what you choose or type (the three answers, “What were you looking for?” of at most 150 characters and notes of at most 500), together with the post and the day. No IP address, browser details or identifier are stored, and nothing is sent unless you press Send feedback. Text containing an email address, phone number or link is refused, so personal details don’t end up in it by accident; if you’d like a reply, use the contact form. When you open the form, it loads Cloudflare Turnstile to check that you’re a person, as on the contact form; Cloudflare also uses your IP address briefly to limit how often feedback can be sent. Feedback is stored in the same EU database as the reading statistics, only I can see it, and it is deleted automatically after 24 months. The legal basis is your consent: you decide to send it.

Stored in your browser

The site stores a few preferences in your browser’s local storage, so they stay the same between visits: light or dark theme (rw-theme), font size (rw-font), high contrast (rw-contrast), reduced motion (rw-motion), whether reading statistics are switched off (rw-stats, only once you change it) and your last few search queries (rw:recent). These never leave your browser. You can remove them by clearing the site data in your browser.

Search, fonts and scripts

Search runs entirely in your browser, using an index that is part of the site. Your searches are not sent anywhere. Fonts, scripts and videos, including the reading statistics script, are served from the site itself, not from a third party. The one exception is Cloudflare Turnstile, which is loaded on the contact page and when you open the feedback form (see Feedback on posts and Contact form).

Contact form

The contact form is handled by a small Cloudflare Worker. It checks the form with Cloudflare Turnstile, a privacy-friendly alternative to a CAPTCHA that loads a script from Cloudflare on the contact page only. The Worker then sends your message as an email through Microsoft 365, where my mailbox is hosted, and does not store it. I keep the email as long as I need it to answer you.

Comments

The old WordPress version of this blog had comments. Approved comments are kept on the posts as a static, read-only archive, showing the name the commenter entered, the date and the text. Email addresses were never published. New comments are not possible. If you wrote a comment and want it removed or anonymised, let me know through the contact form.

Your rights

Under the GDPR you can ask what personal data I hold about you, and ask for it to be corrected or removed. In practice that is only the archived comments: the reading statistics contain nothing that identifies you, and I don’t keep other visitor data myself. For the hosting logs, contact GitHub or Cloudflare. You can reach me through the contact form. You also have the right to file a complaint with the Dutch data protection authority, the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens.
